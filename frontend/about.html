About TriageX

How it works, and what it cannot do

This is a rule-based system, not a trained model. Every decision it makes can be traced back through named rules to the facts of the case. There is no step where a number comes out of something nobody can inspect.

Purpose and intended use

A demonstration of how a knowledge-based system can make a defensible first assessment of a money-laundering alert, and know the limits of its own competence.

What it does

Reads the facts of an alert and recommends one of five actions, with the full reasoning, the rules and sources behind it, and what evidence would change its mind.

Who it is for

Anyone interested in explainable decision systems: compliance and financial-crime professionals, students of knowledge-based systems, and engineers weighing rules against learned models.

What it is not

A compliance product. It has never seen a real case, and nothing it says may be used to make a decision about a real person or account.

It reasons in stages, not in one jump

The easiest way to build something like this is one big table: certain inputs go in, a verdict comes out. That is also the least useful way, because nobody can tell why it said what it said, and it cannot express partial conclusions.

So this system works up through stages instead. Each stage may only read from the stages below it, and a rule is rejected at build time if it tries to skip ahead.

  1. 1

    Measurements

    Plain arithmetic about the case. How many payments, how large, how quickly the money left, how old the account is.

  2. 2

    Observations

    Named things that are true of the activity. “Payments cluster just under a review threshold.” No opinion about what it means yet.

  3. 3

    Patterns

    Recognised laundering methods the observations are consistent with, each with a strength. This is where interpretation starts.

  4. 4

    Assessment

    Is the evidence complete? Is any of this outside what the system can judge? Does a legal obligation apply?

  5. 5

    Risk

    The overall risk posture, combining pattern strength with context.

  6. 6

    Decision

    One of the five actions, chosen by a fixed order of precedence.

A worked chain, from an actual example

The same alert shown on the home page, followed from facts to decision.

Facts
Fourteen cash deposits of £2,400 over six days. Account opened three weeks ago. No evidence on file of where the money came from.
Observations
Deposit frequency is extreme. Each deposit sits just under the £3,000 review threshold. The total is far larger than any single deposit. The account is too new to have a normal pattern.
Pattern
This is strongly consistent with splitting deposits to stay under a threshold.
Assessment
Evidence is incomplete: the source of the money is not documented. Everything else about the case is assessable.
Decision
Request evidence. Specifically: ask for source-of-funds documentation, which is the least intrusive thing that would settle it.

What sets it apart

Three things most decision systems of this kind do not do.

It works out what to ask for

When the system asks for evidence, it has not picked from a fixed list. It searches every combination of evidence the bank could obtain, weighs each by how much effort it takes and how intrusive it is for the customer, and returns the cheapest combination that would actually change the decision.

So a sanctions screen, which is quick, internal and involves no contact with the customer, is preferred over a full interview, unless only the interview would settle the matter. It also reports honestly when nothing would help: if a case involves cryptoasset transfers, no amount of paperwork gives this system a model of how those flows work, so it says the case cannot be resolved rather than inventing a request.

It can explain why it did not do something

The useful question is rarely “why did you say that”. It is “why didn’t you close it”. That cannot be answered by showing the reasoning that was used, because the answer is about something absent.

Every assessment therefore lists each other decision it could have reached and what specifically stopped it, for example: “Evidence on file is Incomplete, and would need to be Complete.”

It checks itself

A separate tool inspects the rules themselves, looking for the sort of defect no individual test case would reveal: two rules that contradict each other, a rule that can never fire, a conclusion nothing is able to reach.

It has found real problems. The most instructive was a rule that recorded a UK public figure as a foreign one. Every case still produced a defensible decision, so no test caught it: the error was in what a fact meant, not in any outcome.

What it is built from

The rules are derived from published material rather than invented: the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002, the Money Laundering Regulations 2017, guidance from the Joint Money Laundering Steering Group and the Financial Conduct Authority, and typology work from the Financial Action Task Force and the Wolfsberg Group. Each rule records where it came from, and is tagged by how directly.

From legislationFor example, a confirmed sanctions match must always be escalated.
From published guidanceFor example, what “splitting deposits” looks like in practice.
ReconstructedThe shape comes from guidance; the numbers were set for this project. This is the largest group, and saying so plainly matters.

That last category is unavoidable. Published guidance describes what suspicious behaviour looks like but deliberately leaves the actual thresholds to each firm’s own risk appetite, so there was nothing to copy. Every number in this system was chosen to be plausible.

What it cannot do

It has never seen a real case. Every example is invented, and every “correct answer” was decided by the same person who wrote the rules. The performance figures on this site measure whether the system is internally consistent. They are not evidence that it would work on real alerts.

Four kinds of laundering are deliberately excluded

Trade-based laundering, cryptoasset ramps, correspondent banking and trust structures. This is not a gap to be filled later: a system that claims to cover everything cannot meaningfully say “this is outside what can be assessed”.

It cannot see intent

It concludes that behaviour matches a pattern, never that someone meant to launder money. Nothing in transaction data shows intent, and claiming otherwise would be dishonest about what the system can see.

It considers one customer and one alert at a time

Real monitoring looks across accounts, across customers and over months. This does not.

Its thresholds are untested for sensitivity

The numbers are plausible, but nobody has measured how much the conclusions would move if they were set differently. That is the largest known gap.

One result that went against the design

The system was built expecting that it should also decline when a decision was simply close, near the boundary between two conclusions. That was implemented and measured, and it made things worse at every setting tested.

The reason is instructive. The system already gets about 98% of the cases it answers right, so declining the marginal ones throws away correct answers about as often as wrong ones.

What does pay is declining for a specific, nameable reason: something outside its competence, or a check that has not been run. In short, knowing what you do not know is worth a great deal. Knowing only that you are unsure is worth nothing.

Terms of use

Short, because the position is simple.

  1. Demonstration only. TriageX is a portfolio demonstration of knowledge-based reasoning. It is not a compliance product and is not intended for operational use.
  2. Synthetic data. Every case, threshold, country risk rating and screening result is invented. Outputs describe the behaviour of the rule base, not of any real person or account.
  3. Not advice. Nothing on this site is legal, regulatory or compliance advice. Decisions about suspicious activity remain with qualified, authorised people under their firm’s own procedures.
  4. Your data. Answers you enter are sent to the assessment service to be evaluated and are not stored there. Your history is kept only in your own browser and can be cleared at any time from the History page.
  5. No personal data. The form asks for no names or identifiers. Do not add any, including in the reference field.
  6. No warranty. The software is provided as is, under the MIT licence, without warranty of any kind.
