Deciding what to do with a money-laundering alert

Transforming complex alerts into focused investigative action.

A bank's monitoring software flags hundreds of alerts a day. Most are innocent behaviour that happened to cross a threshold. Someone has to decide what each one means.

This system does that first assessment. It reads the facts of a case and recommends one of five actions, with its full reasoning, and, where relevant, exactly what evidence would change its mind.

It also does something most systems of this kind will not do: when it cannot justify a decision, it says so and explains what is missing, instead of guessing.

Why refusing to decide is a feature

Under UK law the test for suspicion is objective. Section 330 of the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002 makes it an offence to fail to disclose money laundering where a person had reasonable grounds to suspect it, whether or not they actually did.

What matters, then, is what a reasonable person should have concluded from the information available. A system that quietly turns its own uncertainty into a clean "close the alert" has not removed that uncertainty. It has hidden it, while the legal standard still applies to the firm.

Two different reasons to involve a person

The system distinguishes between them, because they need different responses.

Refer to an investigator The system knows exactly what it is looking at. It is confident, but only a person with reporting authority can decide what happens next.
Cannot decide The system cannot form a view at all: something is outside what it can assess, a required check has not been done, or the evidence points both ways.

Does declining actually help?

It was measured rather than assumed. Across 500 test cases, forcing the system to answer every single one made its decisions 3.5 times more costly once errors are weighted by how much harm they do, because missing a real launderer matters far more than taking a second look at an innocent customer.

A conventional scoring model built from the same signals and the same thresholds did seven times worse, and missed 21 cases that should have been referred against this system's 2.
