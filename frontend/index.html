Five possible decisions

Every alert ends in one of five actions

Two of them involve a person, for different reasons: one because the system is confident, the other because it cannot be.

Why refusing to decide is a feature

Hiding uncertainty does not remove it

Under UK law the test for suspicion is objective. Section 330 of the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002 makes it an offence to fail to disclose money laundering where a person had reasonable grounds to suspect it, whether or not they actually did.

What matters is what a reasonable person should have concluded from the information available. The system therefore distinguishes two very different reasons to involve a person.

Refer to an investigator

The system knows exactly what it is looking at. It is confident, but only a person with reporting authority can decide what happens next.

Cannot decide

The system cannot form a view at all: something is outside what it can assess, a required check has not been done, or the evidence points both ways.

A system that quietly turns its own uncertainty into a clean “close the alert” has not removed that uncertainty. It has hidden it, while the legal standard still applies to the firm.

The design principle behind TriageX
Measured, not assumed

Does declining actually help?

Across 500 test cases, with each error weighted by the harm it does: missing a real launderer matters far more than a second look at an innocent customer.

3.5× more costly when the system is forced to answer every case instead of declining the ones it cannot justify.
7× worse for a conventional scoring model built from the same signals and the same thresholds.
